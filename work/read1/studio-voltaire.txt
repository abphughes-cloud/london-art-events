Katherine Hubbard
 The Great Room
 This major new installation by Katherine Hubbard (b. 1981, Philadelphia, USA) is the artist’s first institutional solo exhibition in the UK. Centring the relationship between Hubbard and her mother, Antonette Berger, The Great Room navigates their encounters with illness, memory, changing identity and loss over a five-year period.  
 Working across installation, performance, writing and analogue photography, Hubbard understands images and our experiences of them as live, ongoing processes. Having avoided photographing other people in her work until now, The Great Room marks a significant departure in the artist’s practice, but continues her interest in representation and the power structures embedded in the act of making a photograph. 
 At the height of the COVID-19 pandemic, Berger began to experience the effects of the progressive brain disease LATE, requiring Hubbard to act as her primary caregiver. Working with two 4×5 large-format film cameras, the two women moved through Berger’s home in Philadelphia, managing the daily routines of domestic life and gradually coming to the painful realisation that Berger’s living situation had become unsustainable.
 In the resulting photographs, the objects and architecture of Berger’s grand but faded house, including mirrors, doorways and unexpected angles, create fractured glimpses and fragmented compositions that undermine the authority of any singular viewpoint or subject. 
 Referencing the walls used by Berger to divide the &#x27;great room&#x27; of her home into a makeshift bedroom, ten partition walls create shifting sightlines as people move through the space. By suspending the photographs and revealing both the front and back of the images, the installation underscores the act of looking as both mediated and physical.
 Throughout the exhibition are several contact prints, created by Hubbard and Berger in a traditional black-and-white darkroom. Coated in vaseline, they touched, leaned and pressed themselves directly onto photographic paper, making impressions with their bodies. Unmediated by a lens, these 1:1-scale works foreground the body as a tool capable of making its own mark, rather than being depicted by a camera.  
 Enabling experiences of loss, memory and changing identity to become tactile and spatialised, The Great Room considers how perspective indelibly shapes us, our relationships and the worlds we inhabit.
 The exhibition has been developed in partnership with Secession [https://secession.at/en/] , Vienna where an extended chapter of the exhibition was presented from 12 June to 30 August 2026. Through this collaboration, the partners have co-commissioned new works by the artist.

Recent solo exhibitions include The great room , Company Gallery, New York (2023); Avoid glancing blows , Company Gallery, New York (2020); Katherine Hubbard , Higher Pictures, New York (2018); stages , Baxter St. Camera Club of New York, New York (2017); and Bring your own lights , The Kitchen, New York (2016). Group exhibitions include Time Management Techniques , The Whitney Museum of American Art, New York (2022); Ecstatic Land , Marfa Ballroom, Texas (2022); A Collection of Slow Events , The Luminary, St. Louis, Missouri (2017); The Artist’s Museum , The Institute of Contemporary Art, Boston (2016); and Greater New York , MoMA PS1 (2015). 
 Hubbard’s performances have been presented at the Chinati Foundation, Marfa, Texas; Konstfack, Stockholm, Sweden; the Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York; and the Whitney Museum of American Art, New York. Her work is included in the permanent collection at the Whitney Museum of American Art. 
 Hubbard is a 2024 Guggenheim fellow in photography and received her MFA in 2010 from the Milton Avery Graduate School of the Arts at Bard College and is currently an Associate Professor and MFA Program Faculty Director at Pratt School of Art.

Bill Walton
 A Form Of Honesty
 This exhibition marks the first solo institutional exhibition of American artist Bill Walton outside of the US, bringing together ten surprising and subtly sentimental sculptural works from across the artist’s quietly remarkable practice.  
 Bill Walton (b. 1931, New Jersey; d. 2010, Philadelphia) spent most of his life in Philadelphia, where he worked as a commercial printer and, over time, developed a self-taught artistic practice. Interested in the materials he used in the process of printing, including wood, lead and steel, more than the finished product, Walton began making sculptures. In his practice, he enacted simple gestures of folding, cutting and stacking, assembling his works by hand using the techniques he had learnt from his trade.
 The sculptural works in this exhibition evidence Walton’s varied and changeable approach to making sculpture. Despite employing the formal language of minimalism, Walton’s interests and influences prove broader and more mischievous. Through small and concentrated works he created illusions which obscured and revealed the world around him. 
 Assemblages of lead, wood, copper, glass, concrete and textile illuminate the artist&#x27;s view that the materials in his work remained subject to change. Materials that appear fixed had the potential to be reworked or prove to be entirely different upon closer inspection. They often appear in unexpected places or in unexpected ways; what appears to be wood may actually be copper, a piece of soft fabric may in fact, have been stiffened with paint. 
 An active and valued member of his local artistic community, Walton enjoyed experimenting with artistic conventions, presenting a carefully crafted piece that would read as a found object, or lighting the space between his works rather than the works themselves.  
 The artist often resisted dating his work, preferring to acknowledge the history and ongoing potential of his materials. Instead of fixing them in time, individual sculptures would be worked on, rearranged, divided or combined; never not in process. The exhibition’s title is taken from an essay by curator Richard Torchia who wrote &quot;When asked about his refusal to assign his works to particular years, [Walton] explains that it is not a strategy to mystify but, rather, a form of honesty.&quot; Titles also served as an important device for Walton, alluding to people, places and sensibilities he was fond of in his daily life. For example, his Sweet Lou and Marie works, two of which feature in the exhibition, serve as a series of portraits of the couple who ran his local diner and became his friends. 
 Each alchemical sculpture, whether a piece of seemingly unremarkable wood spliced with a glint of silver or a piece of fabric that appears soft and malleable but is fixed, reminds us of our continually changing relationship with ‘truth’ and artifice in the world around us. Inspired by commonplace materials and simple gestures, Walton’s practice pursued the intensely intimate, the unknown and the sublime in the everyday.  
 With support from the Bill Walton Exhibition Circle. Special thanks to the Estate of Bill Walton, Fleisher/Ollman Gallery [https://www.fleisher-ollmangallery.com/] , Philadelphia and Sweetwater [https://www.sweetwater.ltd/] , Berlin.
 → About Bill Walton Bill Walton (b. 1931, Camden, New Jersey, USA; d. 2010, Philadelphia, USA) worked as a commercial printmaker and taught at the Moore College of Art and Design (1974–1990). His first solo exhibition took place in 1971 in Philadelphia, where he would have regular exhibitions until his death in 2010. Posthumous exhibitions have been held at the Institute of Contemporary Art, Philadelphia (2011); Drexel University, Philadelphia (2014); and Portikus, Frankfurt (2025).
 His work is included in the permanent collections of the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts, the Philadelphia Museum of Art, the Davis Museum at Wellesley College, and the Yale University Gallery.
